# A timeline you can edit — design

*Written 2026-08-14, nothing built. The design for a Fusion 360-style history
strip: every change recorded as an instruction, stored in the file, editable in
the past, with everything after it recomputed.*

**This is the largest single feature the app has ever been asked for.** It is
bigger than the whole of the 2026-08-14 work put together, and it replaces the
stored half of the undo system and the version snapshots rather than sitting
beside either. Read *What this costs* at the foot before agreeing to it.

**Why this file exists rather than a row in the register.** `open-work` says
*what* each item is and why it matters; this says *how*, which is a different
job and a longer one. The register carries one line pointing here. If a sentence
in this document starts arguing whether the feature is worth doing, it belongs
in the register instead.

---

## The decisions, taken

Seven, all settled with the maintainer on 2026-08-14. They are recorded first
because every design choice below follows from them, and because three of them
chose the expensive option deliberately.

| | decision |
| --- | --- |
| **What it does** | Edit a past step and recompute everything after it. Full parametric, not a viewer. |
| **Where it lives** | In the `.mbrd`. It survives a refresh. Whether it *travels* is asked at export. |
| **Granularity** | Every undoable action, except that a **run** of changes to one card collapses to a single step. |
| **On screen** | A strip along the bottom, opened on demand, **desktop only**. |
| **Broken steps** | Marked red and skipped. The board still builds. Nothing is deleted silently. |
| **Undo** | Drives the marker — but stays **finer than the strip**. A run undoes change by change for as long as it is still in memory. |
| **New work with the marker back** | Truncates. The steps ahead are dropped, the way undo works everywhere. |
| **Saved versions** | Become **named steps** on the timeline. One list, not two. |
| **Sharing** | Export *asks*, and defaults to leaving the history out. |
| **Scrubbing** | Checkpoints are rebuilt when the board opens. Nothing extra is stored. |
| **Trimming** | No hard cap. Past ~2 MB of history, *offer* to trim steps older than 30 days. |

Of these, the last four were settled on the second pass, and two of them —
runs, and undo staying finer than the strip — changed the shape of the engine
rather than only a policy. They have sections of their own below.

---

## What makes this possible here, and what makes it hard

Three facts about this codebase decide the whole shape of the work. Two are
better than expected.

**Only 30 places record an undoable change.** `commit()` is called from thirty
sites outside `history.ts`. That is the entire surface to convert — not the
hundred-odd commands, because most commands funnel through a handful of writers.

**Arrangements are already deterministic.** There is no `Math.random` anywhere in
`arrange/arrangements.ts`. *Arrange as a spiral* replays to exactly the same
board every time, which matters more than it sounds: the computed steps are the
ones where editing the past actually ripples, so they are the ones worth
converting first, and they need no work to become reproducible.

**Imports replay without touching the disk.** Assets are content-addressed and
stored in the board, so a step can say *add the picture whose fingerprint is
this* and mean it forever. In an app that stored file paths this feature would
be impossible.

**What is hard: a change is currently a pair of functions.** `commit(label,
redo, undo)` takes two closures. Closures cannot be written to a file, which is
exactly why undo dies at the refresh today. Every one of the thirty has to become
a written-down instruction instead.

## The spine

### 1. A step is a record, not a function

```
{ op: 'move',   ids: ['k3f9a2'], dx: 40, dy: -12 }
{ op: 'arrange', mode: 'spiral', ids: [...] }
{ op: 'add',    items: [ … ], ids: ['p81m4x', 'q02n8z'] }
```

`board.timeline` is a list of these, oldest first, plus a marker saying how far
along the board currently is.

The board is then *derived*: a starting state plus the steps up to the marker.
That is the whole idea, and everything else in this document is a consequence of
it.

### 2. Creation steps carry the ids they made

`uid()` is a counter plus six random characters. Replay a creation step and the
card comes back with a **different id**, and every later step that names it
breaks — which would make this feature fail on its second step.

The fix is not to seed the random part. It is that **a step that creates things
records what it created**. Replay reuses those ids rather than minting new ones.
This is better than a seed for a reason that only shows up later: it survives
steps being reordered, where a seeded counter would not.

### 3. Sealed steps, or nothing ships for months

Not every action can become an editable instruction at once. Some are genuinely
awkward — a note's rich text, a crop rectangle, a palette extracted from a
photograph.

So there are two kinds of step. An **instruction** carries a rule and can be
edited. A **sealed step** carries the before and after state of what it touched,
replays perfectly, and cannot be edited — it shows on the strip with a small
lock rather than being hidden.

This is the single most important decision in the document. It means the feature
ships when the *engine* is done rather than when every command has been
converted, and commands become editable a few at a time afterwards, forever.

It is also not a novelty. Fusion's **base feature** is the same escape hatch
under another name — geometry brought in from outside the parametric chain, an
imported STEP file or a mesh, which sits in the timeline, replays as itself, and
cannot be edited as a rule. A mature parametric system needed it too, which is
about as much reassurance as a design decision ever gets. Fusion goes further
still and offers a mode with no history at all, on the grounds that sometimes
the recipe is not worth its price.

### 4. Checkpoints, rebuilt rather than stored

Showing step 3,000 by replaying 3,000 steps takes as long as it takes. So there
are checkpoints: a full copy of the board every ~50 steps, and any point is the
nearest checkpoint behind it plus at most 50 steps of work.

**They are not written to the file.** A step is on the order of eighty bytes —
*move these two cards by 40 and −12*. A full board is not: two hundred cards is
something like a hundred kilobytes of JSON. Store one every fifty steps across
three thousand and the checkpoints are roughly **thirty times** the size of the
history they exist to speed up. (Order of magnitude, from the shape of the
records rather than from a measured board.) Rebuilding them instead is three
thousand array operations — small numbers of milliseconds.

**And the rebuild does not delay opening a board**, which was the one real
objection to it. The board you see on open is stored already: `board.json` *is*
the end state, exactly as it is today, and nothing about that changes. The
replay is only needed to scrub, so it happens the first time the strip is
opened, in the background, while the reader looks at it. Until then the timeline
costs nothing at all.

**This is how Fusion 360 does it**, arrived at from a far more extreme version of
the same ratio. A CAD feature is a few hundred bytes to describe and its result
is a B-Rep solid measured in megabytes, so storing the model at every feature
was never on the table: the file holds the feature tree and the end geometry,
and the kernel rebuilds the middle. It costs them exactly what you would expect
— dragging the marker back in a large design is slow, and that slowness is the
evidence there is no dense cache of intermediate states.

**mbrd does not inherit that cost.** Fusion's recompute is slow because
evaluating a feature runs a geometry kernel — booleans on solids, fillet
propagation, a constraint solve. This app's recompute assigns numbers to fields
in an array. Same architecture, three or four orders of magnitude apart, which
is why the small-file half of the trade comes here without the part people
grumble about.

The one thing that would overturn this is a step that turns out to be expensive
to replay — one that has to decode a picture, say. Phase 1 will say, because it
ends with replay running on every test.

### 5. A fingerprint, so a stale timeline cannot lie

An older build opening this file **drops the timeline** and writes the board back
without it — that is already documented for versions. The worse case is subtler:
a build that drops the timeline but keeps the board, edited, and reopened here.
The steps no longer describe the board.

So the timeline stores a fingerprint of the board it should produce. On load,
if replaying to the marker does not match, the timeline is marked **stale** and
shown as unusable rather than trusted. A history that quietly describes the
wrong board is worse than no history.

### 6. A run of changes to one card is one step

Twelve taps of an arrow key are twelve separate commits today —
`commitGeom('Nudge', …)` fires once per keypress at `canvas/input.ts:2376`,
where a whole drag is one. Twelve dots on a strip for one intention is noise,
and the strip is the thing that has to stay readable.

So consecutive changes to the same card, with nothing else in between, are
**one step: the state before the run and the state after it**, and nothing in
between.

- **What collapses:** anything with a before and an after. Move, resize,
  rotate, colour, opacity, corner radius. Not moving alone — a colour slider
  dragged across its range would otherwise flood the strip the same way.
- **What closes a run:** anything else you do. Touch another card, run a
  command, type. Nothing else in between means the run extends, however long
  the gap — a card moved now and moved again an hour later, with nothing
  touched between, is still one step.
- **What it means for editing:** the editable values on a collapsed move step
  are its endpoints. Which is the right thing to expose anyway; nobody wants to
  edit the seventh nudge.

### 7. Undo stays finer than the strip

The one place the two grains deliberately disagree, and the reason the summary
above no longer says undo is simply *replaced*.

`Ctrl+Z` still unwinds a run change by change, exactly as it does today —
nothing about the feel of nudging changes. The strip shows the run as one step
while that is happening.

**Only the collapsed step is written to the file.** The fine trail lives in
memory for the session. So undo is finer today than tomorrow: nudge twelve
times, close the tab, reopen, and one undo takes the whole move back, because
one move is all the file kept. That is the intended trade — it is what keeps the
file the size of the intentions rather than the size of the keystrokes — and it
belongs in the format document rather than being discovered.

Which revises one claim below: `history.ts` does not go away. It **shrinks** to
the live trail — the run in progress and the individual changes inside it —
while the stack, the redo stack, the weight budget and the eviction rules are
replaced by the timeline. The part that survives is the part with no persistence
in it, which is a good line for it to have been cut along.

## Replacing undo

This is the decision with the widest blast radius, and it is a genuine
simplification rather than only a cost.

`Ctrl+Z` becomes *move the marker back one*. `Ctrl+Y` moves it forward. There is
one **stored** history, so there is no way for two files to disagree — and
**undo survives closing the tab**, which it never has before. The live trail
described in the section above sits on top of it and is not a second history; it
is a finer view of the step currently being made.

Three things fall out of it:

**Undo gets slower in one direction.** Stepping *forward* applies one
instruction. Stepping *back* replays from the nearest checkpoint. With
checkpoints every 50 steps that is bounded and small, but it is not free, and
holding `Ctrl+Z` down is the case to measure.

**`history.ts` shrinks to the live trail.** The undo stack, the redo stack, the
weight budget and the eviction rules are all replaced by a list and an integer.
What stays is the run in progress and the fine-grained changes inside it — see
§7 above, which is the amendment to this.

**The phone still needs it.** The *strip* is desktop-only, as decided — but the
history is not, and the phone's undo and redo buttons now drive the marker.
Nothing about that is visible to the person using it, which is the point.

### New work with the marker rolled back: it truncates

Every undo in every application truncates: the steps after the marker are
dropped. Fusion does the opposite — it *inserts* the new feature at the marker
and keeps everything after it.

**Truncate**, decided, because this is replacing `Ctrl+Z` and undo's contract is
universal: a `Ctrl+Z` that kept the future would astonish everybody who has ever
used a computer. Insertion is the more powerful behaviour and stays available
later as an explicit *insert here* action on the strip, where it is something
somebody asked for rather than something that happened to them.

## Saved versions become named steps

The version snapshots built on 2026-08-14 are the other answer to *go back to an
earlier board*, and two mechanisms for one idea is how a person ends up having
to learn which is which. So they fold in: **saving a version puts a label on a
step**, the version panel becomes the strip filtered to the labelled ones, and
there is one list.

**A version stops carrying a copy of the board.** It does not need one — the
timeline can rebuild that point. Which has a consequence worth stating on its
own: the stored snapshots go away, and with them the third member of the
reference union. It goes back to three — the live board, the bin, and the
timeline steps — rather than the four this document assumed before this decision.

Two things get worse, and both are real:

**A version now depends on the timeline surviving.** Today a snapshot stands on
its own; an older build that drops the timeline would take every version with
it. The fingerprint in §5 protects against a stale history *lying*, not against
one being *lost*. This is the strongest argument for keeping the two separate,
and it was heard and set aside.

**Trimming can no longer be blind.** Folding steps older than 30 days into the
starting state would destroy any named version sitting among them. So the rule
is: **a trim never crosses the oldest named step.** Simpler to explain than the
alternative of converting a folded version back into a stored snapshot, and it
follows something true — a person who bothered to name a point said it mattered.

**Existing boards keep their snapshots.** A board saved before this feature has
versions and no timeline, and its versions cannot become steps because there are
no steps to label. So the reader has to accept both forms: a version with a
stored board, and a version that is a label on a step. The timeline on such a
board starts empty, with the board as it stands as the starting state. That is
two shapes to support rather than one, permanently, and there is no way around
it short of discarding people's saved versions.

**This is a deliberate divergence from Fusion**, which keeps both — timeline
features within a design, and document versions of the file itself, separately.
Its versions exist largely to serve cloud collaboration, and this app has none.

## Sharing

The timeline records everything that was tried and thrown away: the rejected
pictures, the layout that was abandoned, the note that was deleted. Send someone
a board with its history and they can scrub back and see all of it.

So **export asks, and the box is off by default.** The file goes out as the board
alone unless somebody says otherwise. Off by default rather than on, because the
failure is asymmetric: forgetting to include history costs a resend, and
forgetting to exclude it cannot be taken back.

Three notes on it:

- **The choice is not remembered.** A preference that quietly turns leaking back
  on months later is precisely the failure this exists to prevent.
- **Leaving it out is not a special code path.** Writing the board without
  `timeline` is exactly what an older build does with a file it does not
  understand, so it is a branch the format already has to survive.
- **The wording has to say what goes with it.** Since versions are now labels on
  steps, exporting without the history exports without the versions too. The
  checkbox reads *Include history and saved versions*, not *Include history* —
  otherwise the honest option silently drops something the person deliberately
  named.

None of this touches the autosaved session, which keeps everything: this is
about the file that leaves the machine.

## Editing the past

The part that makes it a Fusion timeline rather than a fancy undo.

Click a step, change its values, and every step after it replays on top of the
board as it was before that step. The strip shows the rebuild.

**Broken steps are marked and skipped.** A step that names a card your edit means
was never created cannot run. It goes red on the strip, is skipped, and the rest
still replay. Nothing is deleted, and the board still builds — you fix it or
remove it when you want to.

**Be honest about how much rippling there will be.** Most steps in this app are
*absolute*: "put this card here". Editing an earlier step usually will not change
where a later step puts something, because the later step said exactly where. CAD
feels alive because its steps are mostly *relative* — this face, ten millimetres
out.

Which is why the conversion order matters more than the conversion count. The
steps worth making editable first are the computed ones, where a change actually
propagates:

- **arrange** (spiral, grid, scatter, and the rest) — deterministic already
- **align** and **distribute**
- **auto-connect**, which builds a web over whatever is there
- **snap to grid**
- **fit to a paper size**

Convert those and editing the past does something visible. Convert *move card*
first and it will look like the feature does not work.

## The strip

Desktop only, opened on demand, along the bottom edge.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────┐
│  toolbar                         │
│                                  │
│           the board              │
│                                  │
│ ●─●─●─●─✖─●─●─●─🔒─●─│──○─○─○     │  ← slides up when asked for
└──────────────────────────────────┘
                      ↑ marker  ↑ rolled past (greyed)
```

Four things it has to say without a legend: where the marker is, which steps are
sealed, which are broken, and which are ahead of the marker.

**It must not be always-visible**, which was decided and is also forced: the
now-playing player is already pinned to the bottom edge, and `#toolbar` must stay
before `#nowplaying` in `index.html` because the rules that step the player up a
tier are sibling combinators that only look forward. A third element on that edge
joins that ordering constraint, and the CLAUDE.md note about it will need a third
clause.

**The phone gets no strip.** A horizontal list of three thousand steps on a
430px screen is not a feature. The history is still there, still in the file,
still driving undo.

## What goes in the file

A `.mbrd` schema change, on top of the one `versions` just made. The format
document needs a `timeline` section covering:

- the step list, the marker, and the fingerprint
- the two kinds of step, and that a sealed one carries state rather than a rule
- **that a step may be a collapsed run** — endpoints only, with nothing in
  between recorded, and that this is why undo is finer in the session that made
  it than in the one that reads it back
- **that checkpoints are not in the file at all.** They are rebuilt on demand.
  The end state is stored, as it always has been, so nothing about opening a
  board changes
- **that a version is a label on a step**, and that the older shape — a version
  carrying its own copy of the board — has to stay readable forever, because
  boards written before this feature have them
- **the reference union.** A step that added a picture names its fingerprint, so
  an asset a *step* points at is live even when no card on the board wants it.
  The union is three: the live board, the bin, and the steps — versions dropped
  out of it when they stopped carrying copies. Three places compute it — the
  packer, the autosave sweep and the inventory sheet — and all three have to
  learn about steps in the same commit that adds them. This is the part of the
  design most likely to destroy somebody's picture if it is done in two.

### Trimming

No hard cap. Past roughly **2 MB** of history the app *offers* to trim steps
older than **30 days**, folding them into the starting state.

**A trim never crosses the oldest named step**, because a named step is a saved
version and folding it away would destroy it. If the oldest named step is four
years old, nothing trims, and the offer says so rather than quietly doing less
than it promised.

An offer rather than a policy, which matches how the inventory sheet reports
rather than deletes: this is somebody's record of their own work, and the app
quietly throwing away last month is exactly the failure the feature exists to
prevent. Steps are small — tens of bytes each — so 2 MB is on the order of tens
of thousands of actions, and run collapsing pushes that further away again by
storing intentions rather than keystrokes. Most boards will never see the prompt.

## Phases

**In one paragraph, if the rest of this document is ever lost:** build it as a
recipe with the end state cached — which the file already is — and rebuild
intermediate points lazily the first time the strip opens; get the engine landed
in phase 1 with the existing undo running beside it as an oracle in the tests,
because *replay and undo produce the same board* is the only strong check
available in a suite with no browser; ship with sealed steps from day one so the
feature lands when the engine works rather than when all thirty mutation sites
have been converted; then convert the *computed* commands first — arrange,
align, distribute, auto-connect — because they are the only ones where editing
the past visibly ripples, and converting `move` first would make a working
feature look broken; strip last, once there is something worth scrubbing.

Each phase is a commit or a small run of them, in this order, because each one is
useless without the one before it.

**1 — The spine, with no interface at all.** Revised on starting it, and the
revision is the difference between a phase that lands and one that does not —
see *What changed when this met the code* below. The spine is: a generic delta
captured inside `commit()` itself, so all thirty sites become sealed steps
without one of them being touched; run collapsing; the replay engine;
checkpoints; `board.timeline` in the schema and in the format document; the
three reference-union readers; the export checkbox. The old undo stack stays and
runs **in parallel**, and the two are asserted against each other in the tests:
replay and undo must produce the same board. That parallel run is the whole
safety net for phase 2.

Creation ids and op records move *out* of phase 1 and into phase 4, where they
belong: a sealed step already records the ids it created, because it records the
items themselves.

Two things belong in this phase that look like they could wait, and cannot:

- **The three reference-union readers.** The packer, the autosave sweep and the
  inventory sheet learn about steps in the same commit that writes the first
  step. Land them a commit later and the sweep spends that commit deleting
  pictures the history still needs.
- **The export checkbox.** The leak exists the moment a timeline is written to a
  file, not the moment somebody can see one. Shipping the strip with the
  checkbox is shipping it a phase too late.

**2 — Switch undo over.** `Ctrl+Z` drives the marker; the old stack is deleted
and `history.ts` shrinks to the live trail. The riskiest single commit in the
plan, and the reason phase 1 ends with the two agreeing on every test.

**3 — The strip, read-only.** Desktop, on demand. Scrub, and roll the board to a
point. No editing. This is the first phase anybody can *see*, and it is worth
stopping here for a while and living with it. **Versions fold in here**, because
this is where a labelled step becomes something a person can look at: saving a
version labels a step, the version panel becomes the strip filtered to the
labelled ones, and the older snapshot shape stays readable.

**4 — Editing a step.** The recompute, the red broken state, and the value
editors for the converted steps. Start with arrange, align, distribute and
auto-connect — the ones where a change ripples.

**5 — The rest.** The trim offer at 2 MB, with the rule that it never crosses the
oldest named step. Reordering and suppressing steps, and *insert here* as the
explicit form of what truncation refuses to do implicitly, if they still seem
worth it by then. Converting sealed steps to editable ones, a few at a time,
indefinitely.

## What changed when this met the code

Written while building phase 1, because a plan that survives contact with the
codebase unaltered was not looking hard enough.

**Phase 1 no longer converts thirty commit sites. It converts none.**

The original plan said every one of the thirty had to become a written-down
instruction before anything could replay. That is true of an *editable* step and
not true of a sealed one, and sealing can be done generically: `commit()` runs
its redo half itself, so it can take a snapshot of the board on either side of
that call and keep the difference. One change, in one function, and all thirty
sites replay — including every site added afterwards, without anybody
remembering to.

`history.ts`'s own header argues against exactly this, and it is worth saying
why it was right then and is not now:

> a diff would either be enormous or would have to know what every operation
> means. A caller that knows how to reverse itself is cheaper and, more to the
> point, correct by construction.

Enormous is the real objection, and it was aimed at a diff that had to carry
*assets*. It does not: assets are content-addressed and live outside the board,
`item.asset` is a hash, and every field of `Board` is JSON — including
`board.layouts`, which is where the geometry for the layout you are not looking
at is kept. So the difference between two boards is a handful of small objects,
and only for the items that actually changed. The correct-by-construction half
still stands, which is why the old stack keeps running beside this one through
phase 1 rather than being taken on trust.

**What this buys, beyond the time.** Coverage stops depending on diligence.
Under the original plan, a command added next year without an op record would
silently not be in the history, and nothing would fail. Under this one it is
sealed automatically and the worst case is a step that cannot be *edited* — 
visible on the strip as a lock, rather than invisible.

**What it costs.** A board snapshot on every commit. For a two-hundred-item
board that is a couple of hundred small objects to build and compare, on an
action a person just took by hand — nowhere near the frame budget, but it is not
nothing, and it is the number to watch if commits start feeling heavy on a very
large board.

## What this costs

Plainly, because the decisions above chose the expensive option three times over.

**Phase 1 alone is larger than everything done on 2026-08-14 combined.** It
touches the board schema, the file format, all thirty mutation sites, and the id
generator — which is to say, the foundations. Phase 2 replaces the undo system in
one commit.

**There is no browser-driven test suite.** `npm test` can assert that replay and
undo agree, which is a genuinely strong check and the reason phase 1 is shaped
the way it is. It cannot tell you the strip feels right, that scrubbing is
smooth, or that a rebuild does not flicker. Phases 3 and 4 need somebody looking.

**Three things get permanently harder afterwards.** Adding a new command means
adding its instruction record, not just a `commit()` — every future feature pays
a small tax. The reference union gains a member that three separate readers must
all honour, which is the part of this design most likely to cause real data loss
if a later change forgets it. And a version has two shapes forever — a label on
a step, and the older one carrying its own copy of the board — because boards
saved before this feature exist and their versions are not ours to discard.

**The honest alternative, for comparison.** A read-only scrub over the version
snapshots already built is perhaps a twentieth of this work and delivers the
*looking* half of the feature. It was explicitly not chosen. It stays here as
the fallback if phase 1 turns out worse than it looks.
